Abstract
When does a learned local policy execute a distributed algorithm rather than merely solve its task?
We study seven explicitly specified coloring teachers and three neural palette architectures, all with
learned communication. Across heterogeneous protocols, all 21 teacher–architecture combinations have
five frozen checkpoints that each pass 1,000 native functional tests. This finite coverage result combines
twelve historical and nine new certifications; it is not universal correctness. Functional validity and
teacher fidelity separate sharply. For the degree-three algebraic base-case teacher, E1 attains perfect
native action agreement on development teacher states under three procedures, yet mean continuation
accuracy is 22.6404% for passive, 9.2686% for matched, and 99.9932% for counterfactual supervision. The
latter uses more labels, and most sufficient COMMIT input types recur from training. We interpret this as
a paired procedure comparison, not label-matched causal or broad intervention-generalization evidence.
A frozen transfer study reconciles 810,000 confirmatory executions, including 450,000 primary-policy
executions, across degree, size and palette conditions. Fifteen checkpoint–condition cells fail; full-batch
replays distinguish proposal collapse, failed scheduled repair, and incorrect confirmation. Conditional
proofs delimit teacher safety, progress, information requirements and a finite-type MAX certificate. The
evidence identifies what these systems learn, where their rules fail, and which stronger claims remain
open.
1 Introduction
Learning a local algorithm raises a stronger question than terminal task accuracy: under what information,
supervision and input changes does a network reproduce an algorithm’s decisions? Distributed coloring is
useful because validity is easy to check, while anonymous randomness, identifiers, algebraic reduction and
speculative repair yield distinct local rules. The same final proper coloring can conceal different decisions,
trajectories, costs and probability laws.
We distinguish functional validity, action fidelity, transition fidelity, execution-cost fidelity, and equality
of complete output laws. This general distinction is established in imitation learning and neural algorithmic
reasoning. Our contribution is a concrete audited study within one distributed coloring framework. The
central question is: under which declared interfaces can local policies learn the specified rules, what do they
reproduce, and where does frozen transfer fail?
We document native functional coverage of seven variants by three communicating architectures with
all 105 selected checkpoints identified. We expose a large separation between native agreement and typed
continuation responses, auditing initialization, labels, shared heads and evaluation reuse. We reconcile frozen
transfer and reproduce its failures, alongside conditional results separating safety, progress and representation.
We claim neither a new fastest coloring algorithm nor a universally superior training method.
Relation to previous work. Neural graph coloring predates this study (Lemos et al., 2019), as do graph￾algorithm execution with MAX aggregation (Veličković et al., 2020) and trace-based reasoning benchmarks
(Veličković et al., 2022; Veličković and Blundell, 2021). DAgger addresses learner-induced state distributions
(Ross et al., 2011); causal confusion in imitation already motivates interventions (de Haan et al., 2019). Our
fixed-bank teacher continuations are not a new formulation of these general ideas. GNN/local-computation

separates terminal learning from trajectory imitation (Rodionov and Prokhorenkova, 2023). We claim neither
algorithmic intervention nor that distinction as new. Appendix G compares candidate contributions with
precedents.
2 Computational Model and Problem Definition
We use finite simple undirected labeled graphs G = (V, E), n = |V |, d(v) = |N(v)| and maximum degree
∆. Native training uses ∆ ≤ 3 and Cv = {0, 1, 2, 3}. Transfer uses either the global list Cv = {0, . . . , ∆}
or local initial segments Cv = {0, . . . , d(v)}. These are not arbitrary list-coloring instances. Partial colors
satisfy xv ∈ {−1} ∪ Cv, where −1 is pending. Availability is Av = Cv \ {xu : u ∈ N(v), xu ̸= −1}.
A teacher is a specified state machine with observations, private inputs, phases and an interpreter. The
learned policy receives declared observations and emits decisions; the interpreter applies them. Anonymous
trials use two neighbor exchanges; ColorTrials communicates availability-dependent preferences in the second.
JP and Greedy need one exchange mathematically, but their neural decomposition retains two. GM has
supplied blocks, barriers and a serial repair queue; BE supplies algebraic predicates and a reduction clock.
These are distinct computational regimes, not equal-cost anonymous LOCAL algorithms. Message length
and palette computation grow with palette size; we make no CONGEST complexity claim. Recorded native
steps count state-machine applications. BE has two point-reduction steps and thirteen KW steps; neural
point decisions use the first exchange, whereas KW evaluates the second stage as well. Fifteen native steps
are not a fifteen-round learned communication complexity bound.
Mathematical random statements use fresh independent ideal draws. Implementation uses namespaced
PCG64 streams and float32 observations. Required IDs are fixed distinct integers in {0, . . . , 65535}. The
implementation restricts 1 ≤ n ≤ 65536, including anonymous experiments for convenience; this is not an
anonymous-algorithm size lower bound.
Evaluation objects. Functional success means a complete proper in-list coloring within the protocol
horizon and satisfaction of checked native invariants. A cutoff is a benchmark failure, not proof of nontermi￾nation. Action fidelity compares common states/private inputs. Transition fidelity compares updated states;
different action codes need not yield different transitions. Continuation fidelity evaluates a declared oracle
OT (S, a) after a typed message or color-query intervention. Such messages need not be sampled by the native
teacher. Execution costs use teacher-specific units. Distributional fidelity concerns the full terminal vector
and status on a fixed labeled graph. Terminal validity alone does not imply any of the other properties.

7 Information and Identifiers
Consider an initial two-node JP graph with identical anonymous observations and fixed private inputs. Swap￾ping the distinct IDs changes the required root action from write to wait without changing the anonymous
observation.
Proposition 1 (Observation indistinguishability). If two admissible contexts have the same observation
and different deterministic target actions, no deterministic function of that observation is correct on both. A
randomized function cannot be almost surely correct on both.
Proof. Equal inputs give equal outputs or equal conditional output laws. Different point-mass targets cannot
both be realized. For equiprobable binary contexts, average correctness is at most 1/2.
This elementary interface obstruction is not a new distributed lower bound. It requires indistinguishabil￾ity, not numerical closeness, and does not preclude solving coloring by a different rule. Historical integrated
JP/GM already had numeric ID differences; their failures were not ID blindness.

8 Generalization
Teacher extensions. The six non-BE rules admit the parameterized definitions in Appendix A. JP’s
historical cap becomes n, preserving its priority rule. GM retains four blocks/detection/serial repair; Greedy
retains its schedule. Palette lists extend the domain. CT also changes its preference alphabet to {0, . . . , ∆};
it is a declared parameterized variant, not identical fixed-K4 private inputs. BE is excluded because fixed
fields and four-option blocks lack a higher-degree existence proof. Changing those constants would define
another mathematical variant and interface.
Confirmatory design. After a separate pilot, weights, conditions, namespaces and horizons are frozen.
All 150,000 exact-reference executions pass before students run. Ninety primary extensible checkpoints
receive 1,000 executions at each of five conditions: global n = 64, ∆ = 3, 4, 10; local n = 64, ∆ = 4;

9 Discussion
These systems learn executable local modules within a supplied interpreter. Native all-pass packages demon￾strate finite functional learnability, not identification of each teacher’s entire algorithm. BE is informative
because native agreement leaves continuation behavior underconstrained. The budget, overlap and shared￾head audits limit its interpretation to the measured procedures, with posthoc evidence on some unseen local
input types.
Safety and progress. Exact availability and equal-offer rejection preserve partial properness. An always￾wait policy is safe forever. On a K-clique, all nodes proposing one color with independent participation 1/2
progress with probability K/2
K per round. Diffuse independent proposals permit progress bounds, but those
uniform hypotheses are not established for these checkpoints. MAX can implement a presence predicate and
a finite-type COMMIT certificate (Appendix B); it does not establish candidate diversity, termination or
palette-context invariance. All 45 selected canonical transfer cores fail the sufficient all-types condition,
despite successful rollouts; five different historical E1 weights pass conditional certificates. These cohorts
cannot be conflated. Vertex equivariance assumes consistent relabeling of observations, IDs and schedules
in real arithmetic; ordered color features do not imply color-name equivariance.
Output laws and limitations. Properness and one-node marginals do not determine joint coloring laws.
Historical studies use complete vectors/statuses, run-level permutations, fixed graph/checkpoint blocks and
explicit testing families. They are supporting evidence, separate from the 810,000 runs (Appendix F). Nonre￾jection is never equality; coupling and symmetry justify only stated bounds or special cases. Native cohorts
differ in observations, supervision and budgets; combined coverage cannot rank architectures fairly. CF uses
more labels, development states and familiar query templates. Shared heads limit independent architec￾ture evidence. Pilot-selected conditions and paired graph/tape reuse limit extrapolation. Degree, palette
and private-input scale are confounded. Failure witnesses do not settle every capacity/optimization cause.

We freeze scope. A label-matched CF comparison with new final states, held-out intervention families
and paired seeds would address a stronger causal claim, not a prerequisite for the procedure comparison here.
Multigrade retraining, general BE, asynchrony and architecture search remain future work.
10 Conclusion
Seven specified teachers and three communicating architectures attain native coverage under finite hetero￾geneous protocols. Paired continuation diagnostics show that native agreement can coexist with different
teacher responses; resource, overlap and shared-head audits restrict causal interpretation. Frozen transfer
works widely in selected conditions and fails through identifiable proposal, repair and confirmation mecha￾nisms. Conditional mathematical results do not automatically transfer to learned execution. These scoped
findings explain what the systems reproduce and why successful coloring alone does not identify the learned
distributed rule.

F Output Laws and Preserved Distribution Studies
For fixed labeled G, let µπ,G be the full terminal-vector/status law including failures/cutoffs. Labels, palette,
interpreter, cutoff, conditioning and color-name quotient must agree before comparing studies. Equal vertex
marginals do not determine a joint law: equally weighted (0, 0)/(1, 1) versus (0, 1)/(1, 0) have equal marginals
and disjoint support. This logical example is not a proper-coloring counterexample on an edge.
Proposition 8 (Symmetry and coupling). On a complete graph with exactly n colors, a vertex-equivariant
stochastic protocol with exchangeable private inputs is uniform over proper labeled colorings conditional on
successful termination of positive probability. Any coupling (X, Y ) satisfies dTV(L(X),L(Y )) ≤ Pr(X ̸= Y ).
Proof. Vertex permutations act transitively on the n! proper coloring bijections; equivariance and the invari￾ant success event make their conditional masses equal. For any event A, the difference in probabilities of
X, Y ∈ A is at most Pr(X ̸= Y ); take the supremum.
Anonymity alone does not establish the symmetry premises. Untransformed IDs, non-equivariant sched￾ules and numerical ordering require care. With more than n colors, vertex symmetry need not equate
different color subsets. For r independent paired runs of one graph/policy protocol with zero disagreements,
the one-sided upper limit 1 − α
1/r bounds disagreement and hence TV under that coupling. It says nothing
universal about other graphs/checkpoints. Uniform conditional full-transition coupling error ϵ for H com￾mon pre-divergence steps gives dTV ≤ Hϵ; per-node error would also require a node union bound. Native
accuracy is not this uniform premise.
Global-law, local-palette, robust-statistics and factorial studies remain in their historical directories with
schemas, fixed banks and testing families. Complete runs are observations, permutations act on rows rather
than independently on vertices, and witness selection needs an independent split or valid joint permutation
scheme (Gretton et al., 2012; Hemerik and Goeman, 2018; Phipson and Smyth, 2010; Holm, 1979). Reused
banks are historical reanalysis, not independent new confirmation. Nonrejection does not establish equal
laws or tolerance equivalence; power against localized alternatives matters. These studies support scoped
distributional distinctions, not new MMD/permutation theory, and are outside the 810,000 denominator.
